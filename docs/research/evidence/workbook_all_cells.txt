### 09_EXEC_RECOMMENDATION A1:A23
A1: 09 | EXECUTIVE RECOMMENDATION — Board Briefing Note

A5: Executive Summary (3-5 sentences — what does the director most need to know before the meeting?)
A6: Management's board pack will likely present a record year — revenue up 28% to SAR 9.5bn, net income up 14% to SAR 2.1bn and ROE jumping to 58% — and the core digital business genuinely is strong and cash-generative. But FY2025 was a transformational year that the headline numbers disguise: Elm paid SAR 3.39bn to its controlling shareholder (PIF) for Thiqah, a business with only SAR 264m of book net assets, funded it with the company's first-ever bank loan (SAR 1.9bn), and charged SAR 3.12bn straight to retained earnings. That accounting — not better performance — is what drives the ROE spike; on a like-for-like equity base ROE actually fell from 34.5% to about 31%, margins slipped as the lower-margin BPO segment grew, and net cash fell 41% from SAR 3.7bn to SAR 2.2bn. Meanwhile SAR 1.3bn of receivables are more than a year overdue and DSO sits near 140 days — collections, not borrowing, are the largest untapped source of cash. The director should go in focused on whether the Thiqah price is being earned, why its net assets had to be written down by SAR 311m after the interim accounts, and what discipline governs further debt-funded deals.
A8: What Management's Board Pack Likely Understates (top 3-5 findings, plain language)
A9: 1. ROE of 57.7% is an accounting effect, not a performance improvement.
Because Thiqah was bought from a party under common control (PIF), the SAR 3.12bn excess over book value was charged to retained earnings instead of being recorded as goodwill. Equity fell from SAR 5.3bn to 3.6bn, and the DuPont model shows the entire ROE jump comes from the equity multiplier (1.81x → 3.28x). Net margin (24.7% → 22.1%) and ROA (19.1% → 17.6%) both declined. Adding the SAR 3.12bn back, ROE would be about 31% — below FY2024's 34.5%.
A10: 2. Profitability is quietly diluting as the business mix shifts toward lower-margin BPO.
Gross margin fell from 40.9% to 38.9% and EBIT margin from 23.0% to 21.5%. The core Digital segment held its ~47% gross margin, but Business Process Outsourcing (which grew 43%, largely through Thiqah) saw gross margin fall from 24.0% to 18.6%. Net income is also flattered by a ~SAR 69m reversal of prior-year zakat provisions (Note 33); excluding it, net margin is about 21.4%. Part of the 28% revenue growth is acquired, not organic — the pack should split the two.
A11: 3. The balance sheet has moved from a large net-cash position to a leveraged one in a single year.
Elm was debt-free in FY2023–FY2024. It now carries a SAR 1.97bn loan (repayable in five annual instalments from April 2026), debt-to-equity is 0.54x and liabilities fund 70% of assets (from 45%). Cash plus murabaha deposits still look healthy at SAR 4.1bn, but net of the loan they are SAR 2.2bn versus SAR 3.7bn a year earlier (−41%). Interest coverage remains safe at 19x but has fallen from 230x in two years, and the cash ratio (incl. deposits) dropped from 1.08x to 0.74x. SAR 2.4bn of capital commitments and ~SAR 660m of annual dividends compete for the same cash.
A12: 4. A large, quantifiable cash source sits in overdue receivables.
Net receivables are SAR 3.6bn (DSO ≈139 days — roughly 2.5x a normal 50–55 day benchmark). Of the SAR 4.35bn gross balance, SAR 1.31bn (30%) is more than 365 days overdue — SAR 710m government and SAR 597m commercial — and the credit-loss allowance has risen to SAR 748m. Every 10 days of DSO is worth ~SAR 260m of cash; cutting DSO to 120 days would release ~SAR 0.5bn, and to 90 days ~SAR 1.3bn — comparable to two-thirds of the new loan. Unbilled contract assets also jumped 83% to SAR 1.17bn.
A13: 5. The Thiqah acquisition raises related-party and control questions the pack may gloss over.
The price (SAR 3.39bn) was about 12.8x the book value of net assets acquired, paid to the controlling shareholder. After the interim accounts were published, a post-completion review cut Thiqah's acquired net assets from SAR 575m to SAR 264m (−SAR 311m, Note 40-a), mainly in intangibles, receivables, contract assets and CWIP. That suggests the pre-deal due diligence missed material issues — and the valuation paid may rest on assumptions the Board has not yet been able to test against post-acquisition results.
A15: Your Top 5 Questions for Management (expanded list, with the 'why this matters' for each)
A16: 1. We paid SAR 3.39bn for Thiqah — 12.8x its book net assets — to our controlling shareholder. What revenue, EBIT and cash has Thiqah delivered since 30 April 2025 versus the business case, and when will it earn back the price?
Why it matters: With no goodwill on the balance sheet there will be no impairment test to flag underperformance. The Board must track the return on this deal explicitly, and as a related-party transaction it deserves independent scrutiny.
A17: 2. Thiqah's net assets were revised down by SAR 311m after the interim results were published. What was missed in due diligence, who signed it off, and is any price adjustment or recourse available from the seller?
Why it matters: A 54% write-down of acquired net assets within months points to a weakness in deal diligence and financial controls that should be fixed before any further acquisition.
A18: 3. SAR 1.3bn of receivables are over a year old and DSO is ~139 days. What is the collection plan by customer (government vs commercial), with targets and timelines, and why was a loan preferred over accelerating collections?
Why it matters: Collections are the cheapest source of funding available — every 10 days of DSO is ~SAR 260m. Rising credit-loss provisions (SAR 748m) show part of this may never be recovered.
A19: 4. Excluding the accounting effect of the acquisition and the SAR 69m zakat reversal, margins and returns fell in FY2025. What is organic revenue growth, and what is the margin plan for the BPO segment, whose gross margin dropped from 24% to 19%?
Why it matters: The Board needs to know whether the core business is still growing at a high rate or whether the acquisition is masking a slowdown — and whether BPO will keep diluting group margins.
A20: 5. With SAR 1.97bn of debt, SAR 380m+ annual repayments from April 2026, SAR 2.4bn of capital commitments and ~SAR 660m of dividends, what is our target capital structure and the maximum leverage we will accept for future deals?
Why it matters: Elm moved from net cash to leverage in one year. Without an agreed policy (e.g. a net debt / EBITDA ceiling), further debt-funded deals or dividend increases could erode the balance-sheet strength that underpins the business.
A22: Recommended Governance Position (state clearly — consistent with 08_DECISION)
A23: Accept the FY2025 results, but do not approve any further debt-funded acquisition, related-party transaction or dividend increase until management answers the questions above.
Specifically request: (1) a Thiqah post-acquisition performance review against the original business case, reported to the Board quarterly; (2) an independent review of the due-diligence process that led to the SAR 311m net-asset revision; (3) a receivables recovery plan with a DSO target (e.g. ≤120 days within 12 months); and (4) a Board-approved capital-structure and leverage policy before the next major capital decision.
